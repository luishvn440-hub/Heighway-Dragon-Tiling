Tiling principle

Four copies of the dragon curve, each rotated by a multiple of 90°, nestle together perfectly — tail to tail — to tile the entire plane. Harter was the first to demonstrate this in the 1960s.

Rotational symmetry

The four-dragon group has 90° rotational symmetry. Each dragon is placed at 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270°, all sharing the same origin point (the tail). The combined image can then tile the plane by translation.

Lattice grid

For iteration n, the corners of the dragon fall on a square lattice of size (1/√2)n. At even iterations the grid aligns with the axes; at odd iterations it is rotated 45°.

Space-filling limit

As iterations grow, the four rotated copies cover ever more lattice points. In the limit, the tiling is exact and the dragon has area ½ (for unit initial segment), confirmed by the boundary area derivation.

Fractal boundary

The boundary of the Heighway dragon is itself a fractal with Hausdorff dimension ≈ 1.523627. It is the attractor of a 3-function IFS for each of four colored sub-boundaries.

Four components

The boundary decomposes into four self-similar pieces — Red, Blue, Orange, Green — each generated by 3 affine maps. Each piece is a scaled and rotated copy of the others.

Area derivation

From the Level 0 rectangle (area 4a²), each boundary iteration adds a square of side a·(1/√2)n. The infinite sum gives total area = 5a² = b²/2 for initial segment length b.

Dimension formula

The dimension d satisfies x³ − x − 1 = 0 (a cubic from the 3-part IFS). The solution gives d = −ln(x)/ln(√2) ≈ 1.523627, larger than 1 (a curve) but less than 2 (a filled region).

Paper fold method

Fold a long narrow strip of paper in half n times, always in the same direction. Open flat so each fold is a right angle and view from the edge — this is the dragon of order n.

Turn sequence

Each crease is labeled R (right turn) or L (left turn). Order 4: RRLRRLLRRRLLRLL. The sequence is generated by: copy, append R, append reversed-inverted copy.

Non-self-intersection

The paper fold dragon never crosses itself. This was proven by Davis and Knuth (1970) and follows from the fact that the IFS maps produce non-overlapping copies.

Historical origin

John Heighway discovered this construction in 1966. William Harter named it the "dragon." It was described by Martin Gardner in Scientific American in 1967 and each chapter of Crichton's Jurassic Park shows an iteration.

L-system grammar

Angle: 45° · Axiom: FX
Rules: F → Z, X → +FX−−FY+, Y → −FX++FY−

F draws forward, + turns left 45°, − turns right 45°, Z is a no-draw step.

Symbol growth

The string length doubles each iteration (minus constant overhead). At iteration n the string has roughly 2ⁿ draw commands, producing 2ⁿ−1 segments — one for each crease in the paper fold construction.

Equivalence

The L-system and paper fold sequences produce identical paths. The X/Y symbols encode the alternating left/right placement rule, which is equivalent to the recursive inversion in the paper fold turn sequence.

Similarity dimension

The IFS has 2 maps each scaling by 1/√2. Solving 2·(1/√2)d = 1 gives d = log(2)/log(√2) = 2. The dragon is space-filling in the limit — its interior is non-empty.
